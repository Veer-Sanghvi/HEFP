HEFP: Hot Engines and Fire Prevention

What keeps an exhaust manifold from setting things on fire.

HEFP models the heat traveling out of a running engine's exhaust manifold, through a thermal-barrier coating, into the metal wall, and out to the air; then asks whether that outer surface ever gets hot enough to ignite spilled fuel with no spark at all. Every number below is computed live in your browser, using the same equations as the paper. Move a slider and watch it recompute.

Veer Sanghvi · ASTFE TFEC 2027 · read the full paper (PDF)

ELI5

Touch a stove burner and you get burned. No flame required, just heat. Engines have the same problem: the exhaust manifold runs hot enough that if fuel or oil drips on it, it can catch fire on contact. This project asks two things. (1) How hot does that surface actually get, given the gas inside, the coating on it, and the air outside. (2) After you shut the engine off, how long does the danger stick around while it cools?

01. Running engine

02. After shutdown

Post-shutdown heat soak

Turn the engine off and forced convection stops. The only things left cooling the surface are weak natural convection and radiation. Two solutions are compared: a fourth-order Runge-Kutta (RK4) numerical integration of the full T⁴ radiation term, and a linearized shortcut that freezes the radiation coefficient at its hottest value so it can be solved by hand.

ELI5

Right after you shut off a car, the manifold doesn't cool instantly. It stays dangerously hot for a few minutes while the metal radiates its stored heat away, like a cast-iron skillet left on the counter. The question is: how long does that danger window last? The shaded band below is the paper's autoignition range (311–450 °C) for common lubricating oils. As long as the curve is inside that band, a fuel spill on that surface can ignite with no spark.

03. Is the model actually trustworthy?

Sensitivity, uncertainty, and numerical accuracy

Three separate checks, all run live: how sensitive is the answer to gas-side convection, how much does manufacturing tolerance on the coating move the result, and whether the RK4 solver actually converges at the rate it's supposed to (the one check most simulations skip).

h₁ sensitivity

Sweeping gas-side convection from 80 to 400 W/m²K. This is the lever that actually moves the temperature, more than coating thickness ever will.

Manufacturing tolerance (Monte Carlo)

±10% random variation in TBC thickness and conductivity, matching real coating-process tolerance.

▸ RK4 grid-convergence study Does the solver actually converge at 4th order? Expand for the full dt sweep.

A 4th-order method should quarter its error every time you halve the step size (error ∝ dt⁴, so halving dt should divide the error by 2⁴ = 16, an "observed order" of 4). Real solvers rarely show one clean regime across the whole range. This runs the actual sweep, live, against a reference solution 500×+ finer than any step tested.

Three regimes, not one: dt ≥ 64s is pre-asymptotic (order looks too good; higher-order truncation terms haven't been dominated yet). dt ≈ 32–16s is a crossover anomaly where terms partially cancel. dt = 16→2s is the genuine asymptotic 4th-order regime, highlighted below.
